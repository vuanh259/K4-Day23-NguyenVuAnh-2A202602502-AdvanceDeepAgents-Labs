# World Models: From Latent Predictors to Interactive Simulators

## TL;DR

- A useful operational definition of a world model is a learned representation of environment state and dynamics that predicts consequences of actions; PlaNet demonstrates this for image-based control with latent recurrent dynamics and model-predictive planning [1].
- Recent work is broadening the target from compact control-oriented prediction to embodied video anticipation and action-conditioned visual simulation, while evaluation increasingly asks whether models respond usefully to interventions rather than merely generate plausible frames [2][3][4].
- Newer methods combine autoregressive latent dynamics with video decoders, introduce global-state conditioning, or post-train toward geometric grounding; the available evidence is largely abstracts and search summaries, so comparative performance claims remain unverified [4][5][6].
- Long-horizon evaluation remains unsettled: benchmarks propose agent objectives and diagnostics for geometry, interaction, and state evolution, while a 2026 study reports declining goal-conditioned control success with longer offsets even after a dual-latent approach [7][8].

## Background

“World model” is not a single architecture. In model-based reinforcement learning, it commonly means a learned internal model that represents latent state, predicts transitions under actions, and supports planning. PlaNet provides a concrete early formulation: image-based control is treated as a partially observable Markov decision process, with learned transition, observation, reward, and filtering components. Its recurrent state-space model combines deterministic and stochastic latent components, predicts multiple steps, and uses model-predictive control (MPC) with the cross-entropy method (CEM) to choose action sequences [1]. This makes the model useful not only as a predictor but as a substrate for repeated planning and replanning from new observations.

More recent usage often centers on video-generative systems. These systems can render possible futures and may include action or language conditioning, but visual realism alone does not establish that a model captures controllable causal dynamics. The newer literature therefore spans at least three related goals: prediction in a compact latent space for control, generation of future observations for embodied anticipation, and interactive simulation in which an agent can issue actions and assess outcomes [2][3][4]. This survey treats these as overlapping design points, not interchangeable definitions. Evidence below distinguishes methods and reported claims from broader inferences; several recent sources were retrieved only as summaries, constraining what can be concluded about methods and results.

## From Latent Dynamics to Video-Centric Models

The latent-control tradition prioritizes a state representation that supports multi-step prediction and planning. PlaNet’s formulation makes explicit a useful division of labor: encode observations into latent state, predict state evolution, estimate reward, then plan online and update the plan when observations arrive [1]. A compact latent representation can make planning tractable without requiring the model to generate photorealistic observations at every step. This orientation is especially clear when the task is control under partial observability, rather than open-ended visual synthesis.

Recent work extends world-model ambitions toward embodied video. EVA combines video generation and visual-language-model capabilities, using multi-stage pretraining and LoRA adaptation for future-video anticipation in embodied settings [2]. The retrieved summary supports the direction, but not a detailed comparison to latent control models. PAN presents a different combination: an autoregressive latent-dynamics backbone based on an LLM with a video-diffusion decoder, predicting future states from history and natural-language actions [4]. Its stated evaluation directions include action-conditioned simulation, long-horizon forecasting, and simulative reasoning. These designs suggest an emerging division between a structured predictive mechanism and a high-fidelity visual decoder, although the sources available here do not establish whether that division improves control or generality in comparable tests [4].

A related approach uses global-state conditioning in interactive video generation. The paper summary for Learning World Models for Interactive Video Generation says video-retrieval-augmented generation incorporates global-state conditioning to improve long-term consistency and reduce generation errors [6]. This is evidence of a design response to persistent state drift, not proof that the method resolves it. Across these approaches, the central tradeoff is between models optimized for a compact, planning-friendly representation and models that expose their predictions as rich video. The latter are easier to inspect visually, but visual quality is only one component of a useful simulator.

## Action Conditioning and Grounding

Action conditioning is a key boundary between forecasting and simulation. A model that predicts likely future video from context may not respond correctly when an agent deliberately changes its action. PAN explicitly frames prediction around history and natural-language actions, with action-conditioned simulation among its reported evaluation settings [4]. EVA’s retrieved description emphasizes embodied future-video anticipation, but does not provide enough detail to establish how interventions are represented or tested [2]. Thus, the available sources support the importance of the issue more strongly than they support a ranking of specific solutions.

Grounding is another challenge. GrndCtrl argues that high-fidelity video world models may lack geometric grounding, limiting navigation tasks that need spatial coherence and stability. It proposes self-supervised post-training, called Reinforcement Learning with World Grounding, to align pretrained models with physically verifiable structure using geometric and perceptual rewards [5]. This frames grounding as a property that may need to be trained explicitly rather than assumed from visual realism. However, the retrieved evidence is a search summary and does not specify reward definitions, environments, or measured gains [5]. The claim that geometric reward alignment can help navigation is therefore a research hypothesis supported by the paper’s stated motivation and method, not a general result established across settings.

The broader inference is that action responsiveness and spatial grounding should be evaluated independently from image fidelity. A system may produce coherent-looking frames while misrepresenting the effect of a control input or the persistence of geometry. This distinction motivates evaluation protocols that include manipulations, navigational constraints, and repeated state updates, rather than relying on visual inspection alone [3][5].

## Planning and Long-Horizon Use

Planning places stronger demands on a model than one-step prediction. PlaNet uses MPC and replans after observing the environment, limiting reliance on a single long open-loop rollout [1]. This is a foundational strategy for managing model error: predicted consequences guide near-term action, while new observations can correct the trajectory. Recent video-oriented systems raise a harder question: whether a learned simulator remains actionable and coherent over long horizons, especially when the agent’s actions alter the scene.

PAN describes long-horizon simulation as an explicit goal and reports experiments covering forecasting and simulation [4]. The available abstract-level evidence does not give quantitative outcomes, so it cannot establish the degree of improvement or reliability. A 2026 dual-latent world-model study directly examines goal-conditioned visual control at 50- and 100-step offsets. Its abstract reports mean success of 75.9% versus 84.4% at 50 steps and 61.4% versus 69.5% at 100 steps, comparing a baseline to the proposed approach across five tasks [8]. The paper attributes the approach to separating action-conditioned low-level transitions from high-level macro-action planning and supervising predictions at both levels. These figures indicate lower reported success at the longer offset for both compared systems, even as the proposed model improves over the baseline [8]. They are task-specific abstract claims, not a general guarantee about long-horizon reliability.

This evidence points to a useful distinction: horizon length is not just a generation setting but an evaluation variable. Goal success at multiple offsets tests whether errors compound enough to impair action selection; diagnostics of geometry, interaction response, and state evolution can help identify why [7][8].

## Evaluation: Beyond Plausible Frames

WorldSimBench explicitly proposes evaluating video-generation models as world simulators from both visual and action perspectives, pairing perceptual evaluation with implicit manipulation evaluation in embodied scenarios [3]. Its framing challenges evaluations that score only whether generated video appears plausible. The retrieved summary does not provide metric definitions or benchmark results, so it supports the evaluation agenda rather than any conclusion about which models perform best.

PlayWorld takes an agent-centered approach to interactive video world models: multimodal agents pursue long-horizon objectives, while the benchmark evaluates geometry consistency, interaction fidelity, and state evolution [7]. This combines task completion with diagnostics of simulator behavior. Together, WorldSimBench and PlayWorld point toward complementary evidence: perceptual assessment asks what the model renders, manipulation tests whether action has the expected effect, and objective-driven evaluation asks whether an agent can use predictions to accomplish a goal [3][7].

These approaches are promising but do not by themselves settle benchmark validity. The retrieved PlayWorld record is a summary without protocol details, metric definitions, numerical results, or validity analysis [7]. WorldSimBench’s retrieved summary likewise lacks specific tasks and results [3]. Consequently, claims that a benchmark measures “world understanding” should be treated cautiously unless its tasks distinguish visual plausibility from causal response and report reproducible protocols. This is an inference from the evidence gaps in the available records, not a claim that either benchmark is invalid.

## Trends and Open Problems

The last two years represented in the retrieved sources show several converging directions: video generation is being combined with latent dynamics and language or action inputs; global-state conditioning is proposed to reduce long-term inconsistency; and post-training is being used to encourage geometric grounding [4][5][6]. Evaluation is also shifting toward interactive and long-horizon tests, including agent objectives, manipulation, and goal-conditioned control [3][7][8]. Because the set includes 2024–2026 sources, these should be read as visible research directions, not as a complete census of the field.

Four open problems stand out. First, action causality needs stronger tests: does an intervention produce the appropriate state change, rather than a plausible continuation? Second, long-horizon consistency needs evaluation across both visual state and task success; the 2026 results show success falling with larger goal offsets in the reported tasks [8]. Third, grounding methods need transparent protocols and evidence beyond stated motivations, including details of reward signals and environments [5]. Fourth, benchmark claims need full specifications and reproducible measures so that perceptual quality, action fidelity, and agent utility are not collapsed into a single score [3][7].

More fundamentally, world models face a tension between generality and verifiability. A broad video model may cover varied scenes and actions, while a compact control model may be easier to plan with and evaluate in a defined environment. The sources here do not show that one approach dominates. A productive research standard is therefore to report what is predicted, how actions enter the model, how state is represented or grounded, which horizons are tested, and whether evaluations measure rendering, causal interaction, or successful control. That standard follows from the diversity of architectures and the limits of the evidence retrieved, rather than from any one paper’s claims [1][3][4][5][7][8].
